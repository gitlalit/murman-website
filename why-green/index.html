Why green buildings

A green building costs a little more to build and gives more back. This page explains what one is, what it costs and what returns, in plain words, using the terms UN bodies use.

What a green building is

The same building, designed to use less energy, less water and fewer new materials, and to cope with a hotter climate. UN bodies call it a near-zero emission and resilient building. Twelve components do most of the work.

  1. Rooftop solar: makes power on site
  2. Cool roof and roof insulation: keeps the top floor cool
  3. External shading: stops the sun before the glass
  4. High-performance glazing: lets light in, keeps heat out
  5. Daylight and fresh air: fewer lights, healthier rooms
  6. Treated water reuse: flushing and gardens from the treatment plant
  7. Efficient cooling plant: the largest power saving
  8. Low-carbon concrete and blocks: GGBS or fly ash mixes, AAC walls
  9. LED lights with sensors: on only when needed
  10. Meters and controls: what is measured gets managed
  11. Native trees, permeable paving: shade, and rain soaks in
  12. Rainwater harvesting: stored, or recharged to ground

Whole-life carbon has two parts

Embodied carbon

From making the building: cement, steel, glass, blocks, tiles and transport. It is released before the building opens. Cut it by avoiding waste, shifting to low-carbon materials and improving the rest.

Operational carbon

From running the building: cooling, lighting, lifts and pumps. It appears every month in the power bill. Cut it with passive design, energy efficiency and renewable energy.

Why both matter

Buildings and construction account for 34% of global CO2 emissions. UNEP expects embodied carbon to grow from about 25% to nearly half of the sector's emissions by 2050.

What it costs

The extra cost of building green is called the incremental cost. India's first rated building, in 2003, cost 18% more than a conventional one. By 2007 the figure was about 8%. IGBC officials now put it at 1 to 5%, with payback in one to three years. There is no audited Indian average, so the honest answer for any project is a feasibility check on its own drawings.

Where the case is weak

On small plots the certification fees are nearly fixed and the extra floor area is small. Greater Mumbai gives no green FSI. A certificate alone impresses fewer tenants than measured performance. The strongest case today is a large commercial building, leased or owner-occupied, in a UDCPR area.

What the owner gets back

Lower bills

Green-rated buildings use 20 to 30% less energy and 30 to 50% less water than a conventional building of the same size.

IGBC, Green New Buildings

More floor area

In UDCPR areas such as Navi Mumbai, Thane, Kalyan-Dombivli and Pune, a green rating adds 3, 5 or 7% to the basic FSI. Greater Mumbai has no green FSI.

The global direction

The Paris Agreement sets the goal. Each country sets its own contribution: India's latest commits to 47% lower emissions intensity by 2035 and net zero by 2070. At COP28 the Buildings Breakthrough set a shared target of near-zero emission and resilient buildings as the new normal by 2030. India has not signed it, but its energy codes and waste rules move the same way, and our work is aligned with that target.
